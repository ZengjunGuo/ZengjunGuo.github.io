About Me

I am a master’s student in Territorial Spatial Planning at the School of Urban Planning and Design, Peking University, advised by Dr. Junqing Tang.

My research examines the relationships between human activity, urban space, and disaster risk. I study how compound hazards affect critical infrastructure, and how infrastructure disruption interacts with human mobility and protective responses. These questions connect my work on global road systems, urban healthcare, neighborhood accessibility, and disaster early warning.

I combine physics-based hazard simulation and probabilistic risk modeling with large-scale mobility data and geospatial analysis. My current work includes synthetic commuting flows and tropical cyclone wind–rain simulations under climate scenarios. I also study the theoretical foundations of human–environment–disaster relationships. I am interested in using urban analytics and GeoAI to inform climate adaptation and disaster risk reduction.

Research Areas

Human–environment–disasterTropical cyclones and lifelinesResources, access and response

Human–Environment–Disaster Relationships

I explore the geographical foundations of urban resilience by treating disaster risk as an endogenous constraint on human–environment relationships. This work traces the development of resilience concepts, develops a human–environment–disaster coupling framework and examines the marginal constraint effect of disaster risk. The Zhengzhou flood case connects this theoretical perspective with changes in shelter provision, reported disaster locations and emergency response.

Theoretical research · Published in Progress in Geography

Related work on regional resilience uses a dynamic Bayesian network–ARIMA framework across nine Pearl River Delta cities. I refined and applied the framework to examine resilience trajectories and regional differences (Journal of Natural Disasters, minor revision).

Tropical Cyclone Impacts on Urban Lifeline Infrastructure

I investigate how tropical cyclone hazards affect the assets and functions of urban lifeline systems, with applications to transportation and healthcare. My work connects hazard simulation with asset valuation, network performance and the distribution of essential services.

Global road networks and commuting

I investigate how compound tropical cyclone hazards reshape physical road damage and commuting disruption across the world’s urban systems. I generate synthetic commuting origin–destination flows with GlODGen and cyclone tracks from CMIP6 climate environments, then link C15–TCR wind–rain simulations with road asset valuation and network assignment. The study examines where asset losses, disconnected journeys, and rerouting pressures differ, and how these patterns may shift in a warming climate.

Healthcare risk in 52 coastal cities

I assess older-adult exposure, potential hospital asset losses, and healthcare supply mismatches under tropical cyclone winds across 52 Chinese coastal cities. The analysis combines cyclone wind fields with population and hospital data to examine how climate change and population aging jointly reshape risk. By mid-century under SSP5-8.5, annual hospital asset losses are projected to more than double, while climate change amplifies aging-driven exposure growth by 19.7–32.8%.

Under review · Acta Geographica Sinica

Road resilience during Typhoon Mangkhut

I reconstructed Typhoon Mangkhut’s wind and rainfall with ER11 and GMCP and processed 2.5 billion mobile-phone records to examine coastal–inland differences in urban response.

Emergency Resources, Service Accessibility, and Human Responses

I study how the locations of essential services and the movement of people shape flood preparedness and response, linking emergency-resource allocation, neighborhood accessibility and warning behavior.

Emergency-resource allocation across 21 cities

I mapped flood-induced shelter demand–supply mismatches across 21 cities using GHS-SMOD and developed an interactive spatial decision system for emergency-resource allocation. This collaborative work examines how human mobility changes where emergency resources are needed.

Published in Nature Sustainability

15-minute service access during urban floods

I investigate how the spatial distribution of everyday services, flood-induced network disruption, and human mobility jointly shape neighborhood access. Comparing Shenzhen and Zhengzhou, I combine pedestrian-network accessibility models with mobile-phone origin–destination data to distinguish potential access losses from observed changes in arrivals at service-containing neighborhoods.

In preparation

Early warning and protective responses

How do warning timing and urban spatial organization shape protective responses? We compare hourly, 1-km mobile-phone origin–destination flows in Shenzhen during Typhoon Mangkhut and Zhengzhou during the July 2021 rainstorm. The study examines response delays, mobility suppression, and proximity to emergency shelters. I process the mobility data, construct agent-based models of information diffusion and behavioral response, and revise the manuscript, investigating how the population’s initial distribution between home and work affects shelter-in-place and movement patterns.
